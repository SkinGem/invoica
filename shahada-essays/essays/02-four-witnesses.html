Four Witnesses

What counts as evidence here, and in what order it is called

An argument is only as disciplined as its rules of evidence, and most disputes in this territory are not disputes about facts at all — they are disputes about what a given fact is allowed to prove. This essay fixes the rules before the arguments start, so that later essays can be checked against them rather than judged by mood.

Why the order matters

Four kinds of witness are called, always in the same sequence: the Qur'anic text, the Sunna, ancient texts written outside the tradition, and the material record. The sequence is not a ranking of certainty. It is a ranking of relevance to the claim. The claim under examination is a claim the Qur'an makes; so the first task is to establish what it actually says, and only then to ask what supports or embarrasses it.

Reversing that order produces the standard failure of this genre: an inscription or a manuscript is produced with a flourish, and it turns out to answer a question nobody had asked.

First witness — the text

The Qur'an is read first for internal coherence: what a passage says in its own idiom, what the grammar will and will not bear, whether a reading holds across every place the same construction occurs. This is an internal test and it can fail internally. A proposed reading that works in one verse and collapses in three others is refuted without any external evidence being called at all.

First witness · Qur'anic text

وَلَا تَقْفُ مَا لَيْسَ لَكَ بِهِ عِلْمٌ

Wa-lā taqfu mā laysa laka bihi ʿilm.

“And do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge.”

Qur'an 17:36

Taken as a rule of method rather than a piece of piety, this is a prohibition on unearned confidence — including unearned confidence in one's own reading. It cuts inward first.

Second witness — the Sunna, and what a chain can carry

The transmitted reports are called second, and they are called with their gradings attached. This is where most popular argument goes wrong, so it is worth being blunt about the mechanics.

A ḥadīth is two things: a chain (isnād) and a text (matn). The classical science grades the chain — continuity, the reliability and precision of each transmitter, corroboration by parallel chains — and produces a verdict: ṣaḥīḥ, ḥasan, ḍaʿīf, or worse. A report that reaches the threshold of mutawātir, transmitted by so many independent chains that collusion in error is not credible, carries a different weight again.

What a grading licenses

  1. A ṣaḥīḥ report may be used as evidence for the claim it makes.
  2. A ḍaʿīf report may be used as evidence that something was said or circulated, dated to its circulation.
  3. A ḍaʿīf report may not be used to support the substantive claim, and citing one without its grade is not a citation but a rhetorical move.

There is a further limit, and it constrains the arguments in this series directly. The Sunna corroborates; it does not by itself establish a claim the Qur'an is being read to make. Where a later essay leans on a report, the report will be doing supporting work, and the essay will say so.

Third witness — texts from outside

Sources written by people with no stake in the tradition are called third, and they are valuable for exactly one reason: they were not composed to help. Their independence is their evidential value, and it is also the source of their limits — outsiders record what reached them, often at a distance, often with hostility, and always partially.

Third witness · Ancient text

The earliest non-Muslim notices of the movement are Greek, Syriac, and Armenian, and they are close to the events: the Doctrina Jacobi nuper baptizati (Greek, generally dated to the 630s), the chronicle fragment attributed to Thomas the Presbyter (Syriac, c. 640), and the Armenian history attributed to Sebeos (c. 660s).

Cite to a critical edition and translation; the dating of each is itself a scholarly question, and the range should be given rather than a single year

What such sources can establish is that a movement, a leader, and a set of claims were known to outsiders by a certain date. What they cannot establish is the content of a doctrine as its adherents held it — a hostile paraphrase is evidence of the paraphrase. Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing, and arguments in this series that reach for external attestation will be held to the first half only.

Fourth witness — the material record

Last, and answering the narrowest question of all: objects. Manuscripts, inscriptions, coins. Their virtue is that they are not transmitted — they are the thing itself, surviving.

Fourth witness · Material evidence

Manuscript. Two parchment leaves in the Mingana Collection, Cadbury Research Library, Birmingham (Mingana Islamic Arabic 1572a), radiocarbon dated to 568–645 CE at 95.4% confidence.

The range is the evidence, not the midpoint — and radiocarbon dates the parchment's animal, not the act of writing on it, which sets an earliest bound and no latest one

Fourth witness · Material evidence

Inscription. The mosaic inscriptions of the Dome of the Rock, Jerusalem, dated by their own text to 72 AH (691–692 CE): monumental, public, and epigraphically fixed in place, carrying formulae of testimony at a datable moment.

A dated inscription is one of the few sources that carries its own timestamp; what it fixes is public declaration, not private belief

The discipline required here is simple and constantly violated. A manuscript establishes that a text existed in a certain form by a certain date. An inscription establishes that a formula was publicly proclaimed by a certain date. A coin establishes what a state was willing to strike into currency. None of them establishes that the claim written on them is true. Material evidence bears on transmission and chronology. It is mute on metaphysics, and asking it to speak there is a category error dressed up as rigour.

Precedence, and what happens in a conflict

Witnesses disagree. When they do, these rules apply, and they apply whether or not the result is congenial:

  • An internal failure is decisive. If a reading of the text collapses under the text's own grammar or usage, no external support rescues it.
  • Material evidence outranks report on questions of date. A dated object beats a transmitted claim about when something happened, because that is precisely what the object is good for.
  • Report outranks material evidence on questions of content. An inscription's wording tells you what was inscribed, not what was meant by those who held the doctrine.
  • A single hostile source does not defeat a well-attested one, and a single friendly source does not establish a contested one. Weight tracks independence and proximity, not agreeableness.
  • Unresolved conflict is reported as unresolved. The temptation to harmonise is the strongest one in this field and produces the least durable work.

What this method does not give you

It does not give certainty, and it is not designed to. Four witnesses in agreement give a well-supported claim, which is a different and more honest thing. Where they diverge, later essays will say where, and will not pretend the divergence away.

The point of setting the rules out in advance is that they can now be used against the essays that follow. That is not a risk to the project. It is the project.

Notes

  1. Datings, shelfmarks, and confidence intervals given above should be checked against the holding institution's own published description before the essay is finalised; where scholarship disputes a dating, the dispute belongs in the text rather than in a note.
  2. The gradings referred to in section iii are those of the classical ḥadīth sciences. Where a modern scholar dissents from a classical grading, this series states both and says which it is using.
