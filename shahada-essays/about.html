About

What this is, how it argues, and where it stops.

The question

These essays take a single sentence seriously as a claim: lā ilāha illā Allāh. Not as a slogan, not as an identity marker, but as a proposition with a logical form, entailments, and consequences that can be stated clearly enough to be argued against.

That is an unusual way to treat it, and it is deliberate. A claim that is never examined is not believed so much as inherited. The Qur'an itself repeatedly demands proof — of others, and by implication of its own readers — and rebukes the pursuit of what one has no knowledge of. Taking that demand at face value is the whole method.

The four witnesses

Each essay calls the same witnesses in the same order, and the order carries weight. The Qur'anic text comes first and is read for internal coherence before anything external is brought to it. The Sunna is called second, with chains graded and the grading stated — a report's strength is part of the argument, not a footnote to it. Ancient and non-Islamic texts come third, valuable precisely because they were not written to support the case. Material evidence comes last and answers a narrower question than it is usually asked: manuscripts, inscriptions, and coinage can establish that something was written, held, and publicly declared by a certain date, and they cannot establish that it was true.

That last distinction is not a hedge. It is the difference between evidence and the thing evidence is for, and collapsing it is the most common failure in this genre.

Rules the essays hold to

  • No unsourced strength. A ḥadīth is cited with its grading, or it is not cited as support.
  • No borrowed certainty. Where a source is contested, the contest is described before the source is used.
  • Dates carry methods. A radiocarbon range is given as a range, with its confidence interval, not as a year.
  • Objections get their best form. An argument that only defeats a weak version of the counter-position has not been tested.
  • Silence is reported. Where the evidence simply does not reach, that is written down rather than filled in.

Where this stops

Nothing here is a fatwā, and nothing here should be mistaken for one. These essays make no rulings on practice, settle no disputes between schools, and speak for no institution. They are works of argument by a reader, and they are open to correction by anyone who can show the argument fails — which is, after all, what asking for proof commits you to accepting.

Corrections

Errors of citation, transmission, translation, or reasoning are worth more to this project than agreement is. If you find one, the correction is welcome and will be made in the text with a note.
